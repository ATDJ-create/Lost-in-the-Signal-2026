THE SCIENCE

How GPS Works

Simple explanations behind our three experiments.

Back to experiments

1. Finding a location

Satellites send signals containing information about their position and timing. A receiver uses the signals to estimate its distance from each satellite. In our classroom model, each measured distance draws a circle around a satellite. The receiver is where the circles meet when the measurements are exact. The satellites shown are fictional points on a flat classroom map, not real satellite orbits.

We use Pythagoras' theorem: distance = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²). Three suitable exact distance measurements can determine a position on our flat map. Actual GPS works in three dimensions and normally needs at least four satellites to also solve for receiver clock error.

2. Timing errors

GPS signals travel at approximately the speed of light: 299,792,458 metres per second. The relationship is distance = speed × time. A delay of 100 nanoseconds adds approximately 30 metres to a measured range. It does not necessarily create 30 metres of position error because the final position depends on the satellite arrangement and all measurements. Our website uses a best-fit estimate when four distance circles do not meet at one point.

Real GPS can also be affected by buildings, atmospheric conditions and reflected signals. Our model adds a delay to just one satellite measurement and is not a full simulation of these effects.

3. Route planning

GPS estimates the receiver's position. Navigation software then chooses roads. Our fictional city uses a grid of junctions and a breadth-first search algorithm. It explores neighbouring junctions in layers. When every street segment has the same length, it finds a shortest available route. Try East Market to see a road closure increase the number of segments.

Real navigation software uses more complicated road networks and may consider travel times, traffic, speed limits and restrictions.

What our model cannot show

Our satellite diagrams use a flat map, simplified satellite positions and known distances. The signal experiment assumes one map unit equals one metre for illustration. The city map is fictional. These choices make the science understandable without claiming that this website is a real GPS receiver.
