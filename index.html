THE MYSTERY BEHIND GPS

How can satellites find us?

We use GPS almost every day, whether we're finding directions, tracking a delivery or travelling somewhere new. But have you ever wondered how it actually works?

GPS uses signals from satellites orbiting Earth to help calculate our location. However, these signals aren't always perfect.

We created Lost in the Signal to explore how GPS works, why it sometimes makes mistakes, and how those mistakes can affect navigation.

Start with one satellite

Why do real GPS systems normally need four satellites?

Our map is a 2D classroom model: with three suitable distance measurements, we can locate a point on a flat map. Real GPS must find latitude, longitude, altitude and correct the receiver's clock error. That means four unknown values, so at least four suitable satellite measurements are normally required.

In this experiment, the distances are assumed to be exact. In the next experiment, we'll investigate what happens when signals are delayed.

Why does time matter?

Distance = speed × time. Light travels about 0.30 metres in one nanosecond, so a 100 ns delay adds about 30 metres to one measured distance. That does not necessarily mean the final position is wrong by 30 metres.

Our classroom map uses scaled distances so that the error is visible. Real GPS also needs to correct receiver clock error and other effects.

How does the route finder work?

We use breadth-first search (BFS). It checks roads one step at a time, starting with the nearest junctions. Because every road segment has the same length in this model, the first route found is a shortest available route. Real navigation apps also consider traffic, speed limits and travel times.

GPS estimates where you are; a route-finding algorithm decides which roads to take. These are two different jobs working together.
